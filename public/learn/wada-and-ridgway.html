Wada and the Bird Man
Where the English names in Wada’s dictionary come from

Wada and the Bird Man

Leaf through the English edition of Wada’s dictionary and the names start to feel curiously specific, with colours such as Hay’s Russet, Kronberg’s Green and Cotinga Purple. Most of them come from a single book: Color Standards and Color Nomenclature, published in 1912 by the American ornithologist Robert Ridgway. Ridgway wanted naturalists to be able to describe a bird’s plumage precisely, so he gathered 1,115 carefully named colour swatches into one guide. This page compares all 159 of Wada’s names with his list.

The colours match as well as the names

If the English names had simply been borrowed as labels, the colours behind them could have been anything. In fact, they match. The chart below compares each of Wada’s colours with Ridgway’s swatch of the same name and shows how different the two look. It uses a standard measure of colour difference, where 0 means identical and anything under about 5 looks alike at a glance.

Twelve names copied with a slip

Twelve names in the English edition are near misses of Ridgway’s spellings, such as Pomegranite for Pomegranate and Antwarp for Antwerp. They look like slips made while copying from his list, which is good evidence that someone had Ridgway’s book open at the time.

What we don’t know

Nobody knows for certain who chose the English names. They may come from Wada’s original volumes in the 1930s, or they may have been added when Seigensha republished the book in 2010. Wada founded the Japan Standard Color Association in 1927, and Ridgway’s book was one of the most widely used colour standards of the day, so it would have been a natural reference for him. It’s a likely story, but not a proven one.

The 23 names with no Ridgway match are mostly everyday words, such as Red, Blue, Yellow Orange, Khaki and Ecru. They look like plain English labels for colours that Ridgway didn’t name.

The birds, and the rest of nature

Ridgway spent most of his working life as curator of birds at the Smithsonian. His colour names spread through field guides and museum catalogues, where a bird’s breast might be described as “cinnamon-buff” or its back as “Hay’s russet”. One of Wada’s names even belongs to a bird: Cotinga Purple, after the cotingas of Central and South America.

Every colour, side by side

In each pair, the left square is Wada’s colour and the right one is Ridgway’s swatch of the same name, taken from scans of the 1912 plates. Old paper fades, so many of Ridgway’s swatches look duller today than when they were first printed.

How this was worked out: names were compared ignoring punctuation and capital letters, and spelling slips were checked by hand. Colour differences use CIEDE2000, a standard way of measuring how different two colours look. The “same colour word” comparison measures each Wada colour against every other Ridgway colour whose name shares a colour word, such as green, buff or olive, and takes the middle value.
